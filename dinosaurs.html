How Science Knows
What Dinosaurs Did

Fossil evidence, isotope chemistry, and comparisons with living relatives — here's how paleontologists reconstruct the lives of animals that vanished 66 million years ago.

The Science Toolkit

Paleontologists never actually see a dinosaur alive — but they have a surprisingly powerful set of tools to reconstruct behaviour from bones, chemistry, and living relatives.

🦴

Bone Histology

Cutting thin slices of fossil bone under a microscope reveals growth rings (like tree rings), metabolic rate, age at death, and signs of injury or disease.

Fossil Analysis

Key Evidence

Fibrolamellar bone tissue — found in Tyrannosaurus, sauropods, and hadrosaurs — is identical to that in modern fast-growing endothermic (warm-blooded) animals like birds and mammals. This suggests many dinosaurs had high metabolisms and grew quickly.

  • T. rex reached adult size in ~20 years (Erickson et al., 2004, Nature)
  • Bone rings show Maiasaura hatchlings grew ~1.4 m in their first year
  • Stress fractures in predator feet imply high-speed activity

📖 Horner & Padian (2004) "Dinosaur Physiology"; Erickson et al. (2004) Nature 430

⚗️

Isotope Geochemistry

Ratios of carbon, oxygen, and strontium isotopes locked in tooth enamel and bone mineral reveal diet, body temperature, and even migration patterns.

Geochemistry

Key Evidence

Carbon isotope ratios (¹³C/¹²C) in tooth enamel differ between plant-eaters that fed on C3 (forest) vs C4 (grassland-like) vegetation, identifying seasonal migration. Oxygen isotopes (¹⁸O/¹⁶O) indicate body temperature — showing sauropods ran at ~36–38°C, close to modern mammals.

  • Strontium (⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr) ratios proved some sauropods migrated hundreds of kilometres (Fricke et al., 2011)
  • Clumped isotope thermometry on T. rex teeth: ~38–40°C body temperature (Eagle et al., 2011)

📖 Eagle et al. (2011) Science; Fricke & Rogers (2000) Geology

🐾

Trace Fossils & Trackways

Footprints, nesting sites, burrows, and coprolites (fossilised dung) record behaviour directly — no inference needed.

Trace Fossils

Key Evidence

Sauropod trackways in Texas show herding: multiple animals of the same species walking in the same direction at the same time. Theropod tracks with sudden direction changes suggest active pursuit predation. Oviraptorid nests with adults curled over eggs (like brooding birds) confirm parental care.

  • Maiasaura (Montana) nesting colonies: young with worn teeth stayed in the nest = parental feeding (Horner, 1979)
  • Coprolites from large theropods contain crushed bone, confirming bone-crushing feeding (Chin et al., 1998)
  • Cretaceous polar trackways imply seasonal migration toward light

📖 Horner & Makela (1979) Nature; Chin et al. (1998) Nature 393

🐦

Phylogenetic Bracketing

Birds are living dinosaurs (Avian dinosaurs). Crocodilians are their closest living non-avian relatives. Behaviours shared by both groups were almost certainly present in non-avian dinosaurs.

Modern Relatives

Key Evidence

Both birds and crocodilians build nests, guard eggs, and vocalise to communicate with young. Because dinosaurs sit between these two groups on the evolutionary tree, the same behaviours are inferred for non-avian dinosaurs — this is called the extant phylogenetic bracket (EPB).

  • Brooding posture in Oviraptor and Citipati — identical to modern ostriches
  • Air sacs in theropod ribs (bird-like respiratory system) inferred from vertebral pneumaticity
  • Croc-like vocalisation cavities found in hadrosaur crests

📖 Witmer (1995) "The Extant Phylogenetic Bracket"; Clark et al. (1999)

🧬

Soft Tissue & Proteins

Exceptionally preserved specimens yield melanosomes, proteins, and even traces of DNA degradation products — allowing colour and physiology reconstruction.

Biomolecules

Key Evidence

Melanosomes are microscopic organelles that produce colour. Fossilised melanosomes in feathered dinosaurs and early birds preserve their shape — rod-shaped = eumelanin (black/brown), spherical = phaeomelanin (reddish/orange). This gives us actual colour patterns.

  • Microraptor: iridescent black plumage (Xu et al., 2012, Science)
  • Anchiornis: black-and-white wings with a rusty-orange crest
  • Collagen proteins recovered from T. rex bone (Schweitzer et al., 2007) — structurally similar to chicken collagen

📖 Xu et al. (2012) Science; Schweitzer et al. (2007) Science 316

💻

Biomechanical Modelling

CT scans of skulls and limbs feed into computer models that calculate bite force, running speed, and sensory capability from pure physics.

Behaviour

Key Evidence

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) applies forces to a 3D digital skull and reveals stress concentrations — showing whether a dinosaur's jaws were built for crushing bone, slicing flesh, or stripping vegetation. Muscle attachments reconstructed from bone scars feed the model.

  • T. rex bite force: ~57,000 N — enough to crush bone (Bates & Falkingham, 2012)
  • Spinosaurus limb proportions + density: semi-aquatic wading predator (Ibrahim et al., 2020)
  • Hadrosaur inner ear geometry matches animals that perceive low-frequency sound — consistent with social vocalisation

📖 Bates & Falkingham (2012) Biol. Letters; Ibrahim et al. (2020) Nature

What Did Dinosaurs Eat?

Diet is one of the best-understood aspects of dinosaur biology, because teeth, jaw mechanics, stomach contents, and coprolites all leave direct evidence.

🔍

Among all known dinosaur species, roughly 65% were herbivores, ~30% were carnivores, and ~5% were omnivores or had specialised diets.

🌿 Herbivores~65% of known species
🥩 Carnivores~30% of known species
🌰 Omnivores / Specialists~5% of known species

🌿

Sauropods — The Giant Browsers

Long necks, pencil-like or spoon-shaped teeth, and enormous gut cavities. Isotopes confirm they fed at multiple canopy heights and migrated for food.

Herbivore

The Evidence

Sauropod teeth are never serrated and lack the carnivore's cutting edge — they were rakes for stripping foliage, not chewing. Their gut was enormous (fermentation chamber) to extract energy from tough Jurassic vegetation like ferns and cycads. Wear patterns on teeth differ by species: broad spoon teeth = softer plants; pencil teeth = tougher conifers.

  • Brachiosaurus tooth wear: 90% abrasion from high, coarse vegetation
  • Diplodocus fed near-ground on soft ferns (low-angle neck posture from FEA)
  • Carbon isotopes confirm seasonal movement between feeding zones
🥩

Tyrannosaurus rex — Predator & Scavenger?

Massive serrated teeth, binocular vision, bone-crushing bite, and a powerful olfactory bulb. The great debate: active hunter or mostly scavenger?

Carnivore

The Evidence — Mostly Hunter

Forward-facing eyes gave T. rex stereoscopic vision — only needed for judging distance to moving prey. Pure scavengers (like vultures) have wide panoramic vision. The olfactory bulb was enormous — 67% of total brain volume — enabling detection of prey or carcasses from km away. Healed bite marks on Triceratops bones prove T. rex attacked living animals (it healed after).

  • Triceratops ilium with healed T. rex bite: prey survived at least one attack (Happ, 2008)
  • Bite force ~57,000 N: capable of crushing bones to extract marrow (opportunistic scavenging confirmed)
  • Consensus today: primarily active predator, opportunistic scavenger when possible
🦷

Hadrosaurs — Duck-Billed Grazers

Up to 1,400 teeth in a dental battery — the most complex teeth of any vertebrate. Stomach contents found in 3 specimens preserve the actual last meal.

Herbivore

The Evidence

The mummified hadrosaur "Leonardo" preserved gut contents: conifer needles, seeds, ferns, and flowering plant material. Hadrosaur dental batteries self-sharpened as old teeth were replaced — an adaptation for continuously processing abrasive vegetation, similar to elephant teeth.

  • "Leonardo" gut contents: diverse plant diet including hardwoods (Chin et al., 2003)
  • Isotope ratios: some populations fed near freshwater (aquatic plants)
  • 1,400-tooth battery replaced continuously throughout life
🦅

Velociraptors — Pack Hunters?

Sickle claw, grasping hands, stereoscopic vision — built for attacking live prey. But did they hunt in packs? The evidence is more ambiguous than the movies suggest.

Carnivore

The Evidence

The famous "Fighting Dinosaurs" fossil from Mongolia shows a Velociraptor locked in combat with a Protoceratops — proof of active predation on prey larger than itself. The sickle claw was likely used to pin struggling prey (pin-and-bite, like modern raptors), not to slash. Pack hunting is inferred from trackways and comparisons with social modern birds — but direct evidence is limited.

  • "Fighting Dinosaurs" specimen: direct predation evidence (Kielan-Jaworowska & Barsbold, 1972)
  • Sickle claw: no wear = not for slashing; used to grip prey (Manning et al., 2009)
  • Feathered (quill knobs found on arm bones): kept body temperature stable for active hunting
🐟

Spinosaurus — The Fish-Eater

Long crocodile-like snout, conical non-serrated teeth, dense bones for buoyancy control, and isotopes matching a semi-aquatic lifestyle.

Specialist

The Evidence

Spinosaurus's oxygen isotope ratios match modern semi-aquatic animals (crocodilians, hippos) rather than terrestrial dinosaurs. Conical teeth without serrations are ideal for catching slippery fish. Stomach contents of a related spinosaurid (Baryonyx) contained scales and bones of large fish plus a young Iguanodon — showing dietary flexibility.

  • Baryonyx stomach: Lepidotes fish scales + juvenile Iguanodon bones (Charig & Milner, 1997)
  • Dense Spinosaurus femur: reduces buoyancy for shallow-water wading (Ibrahim et al., 2020)
  • Isotope signature matches semi-aquatic lifestyle (Amiot et al., 2010, Nature)
🥚

Oviraptors — The Misunderstood Omnivores

Named "egg thief" — but the eggs they were caught on were their own. Toothless beaks and powerful jaw muscles suggest a varied diet including eggs, plants, and invertebrates.

Omnivore

The Evidence

Oviraptorids were first found on a nest of eggs assumed to belong to the nearby Protoceratops — hence "egg thief." Later discoveries found oviraptorids sitting on those same eggs in a brooding posture identical to birds. The eggs were theirs. Jaw mechanics suggest crushing hard food items (molluscs, hard seeds, or bones).

  • Brooding Citipati specimens: arms spread over eggs in avian wing-spread posture
  • Jaw joint geometry: ~300 N bite force — adequate for crushing invertebrates
  • Some specimens found with lizard bones inside body cavity

Did Dinosaurs Have a Mating Dance?

The short answer: almost certainly yes. Here is the direct fossil evidence and what it tells us about dinosaur courtship.

🎯

The 2016 discovery of large dinosaur "scrape sites" in Colorado directly confirmed that large theropods performed ground-scraping courtship displays — the same behaviour seen in modern birds like puffins and ostriches. It was the first direct physical evidence of dinosaur mating behaviour.

🏃

Ground-Scraping Display

In 2016, palaeontologists found over 50 large (<1 m wide) scrapes carved into sandstone at four sites in Colorado, dated to the Late Cretaceous. The marks match those made by large theropods and are clustered in "lek" formations — exactly like modern birds that gather at display arenas.

📍 Lockley et al. (2016), Scientific Reports — "Theropod courtship: large scale physical evidence of display arenas"
🎨

Colourful Crests & Frills

Many dinosaurs had elaborate head structures with no clear defensive or feeding function: Dilophosaurus crests, Parasaurolophus crests, Triceratops frills. Blood vessel channels in frills suggest they flushed with colour — like the dewlaps of modern chameleons and iguanas, used in mate selection.

Bone texture + vascular channels in Triceratops frills: Scanella & Horner (2010). Melanosome-based iridescent feathers in Microraptor: Xu et al. (2012).
📢

Hollow Crests as Sound Resonators

Parasaurolophus had a 1.8 m hollow bony crest connected directly to its nasal passage. Computer models show it resonated at 30–48 Hz — a deep, carrying sound similar to a trombone. Since both birds and crocs use vocalisation in courtship, this was almost certainly used for mate calling.

CT-scanned crest resonance model: Weishampel (1981) Paleobiology; updated Diegert & Williamson (1998). Phylogenetic bracket: both avian and croc relatives vocalise during mating.
💪

Sexual Dimorphism in Horns & Spines

In many ceratopsian species (Triceratops, Pachyrhinosaurus), horn and frill shapes vary significantly within the same species. Statistical analysis suggests two morphs — interpreted as male and female. Elaborate horns in males are consistent with sexual selection, just as in deer and rhinoceros.

Dodson (1976) first described ceratopsian dimorphism; Mallon (2017, PLOS ONE) used discriminant analysis to confirm two morphological groups.
🪶

Feather Displays in Feathered Dinosaurs

Microraptors, Anchiornis, and other paravian dinosaurs had strikingly patterned plumage reconstructed from melanosomes. High-contrast patterns (white wing patches, coloured crests) are classic honest-signal displays in birds — indicating fitness to potential mates, exactly as in modern peacocks or birds-of-paradise.

Anchiornis colour reconstruction: Li et al. (2010) Science. Microraptor iridescence: Xu et al. (2012) Science.
🦚

The Bird Comparison

Birds ARE dinosaurs — they are the only surviving branch of theropod dinosaurs. Every mating display in modern birds (dancing, singing, nest-building, colour display) is direct evidence that these behaviours existed in their ancestors. The question is not whether non-avian dinosaurs displayed — it is how elaborate it was.

Gauthier (1986) established the theropod-bird link; modern consensus: ~10,000 bird species = 10,000 living dinosaur species.

Key Discoveries That Changed Everything

Palaeontology has transformed from bone-collecting into a multi-disciplinary science. Here are the milestones that rewrote what we know about dinosaur behaviour.

1842

Richard Owen Coins "Dinosauria"

Recognised Megalosaurus, Iguanodon, and Hylaeosaurus as a distinct group. Early concept: slow, cold-blooded, lizard-like monsters.

1964

Deinonychus Changes Everything

John Ostrom's discovery of this agile predator sparks the "Dinosaur Renaissance" — the shift from viewing dinos as sluggish reptiles to active, warm-blooded animals.

1978

Maiasaura — Proof of Parental Care

Jack Horner finds colonial nesting sites in Montana: young with worn teeth stayed in the nest, proving adults brought food. The "good mother lizard."

1993

Fighting Dinosaurs Reinterpreted

The Velociraptor-Protoceratops specimen is confirmed as genuine prey-predator combat, not a chance burial — direct behavioural evidence.

1996

First Feathered Dinosaur — Sinosauropteryx

Simple filamentous feathers on a small theropod from China prove the bird-dinosaur link. The colour of those feathers is later reconstructed from melanosomes.

2007

T. rex Proteins Recovered

Mary Schweitzer extracts collagen from T. rex bone — structurally closest to chickens. Confirms birds as closest living relatives.

2010

Dinosaur Colour Reconstruction

Melanosome analysis gives Anchiornis a precise colour pattern for the first time: black-and-white wings, rusty head crest. Patterning consistent with sexual display.

2016

First Direct Evidence of Mating Display

Lockley et al. describe 50+ ground-scrape structures in Colorado — identical to modern bird courtship "lek" displays. The first physical evidence of dinosaur courtship behaviour.

2020

Spinosaurus Was Semi-Aquatic

New Spinosaurus material from Morocco combined with isotope data confirms it lived in and hunted in water — the first confirmed aquatic non-avian dinosaur.
